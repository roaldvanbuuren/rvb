The system is not rigged against you. In fact, my belief is that we tried to make a system so fair for everyone that it ends up being fair to no one.
I don't think there are evil people or institutions, like the EU or governments, that are trying to make your life more difficult. Sure, there will be people who are there to serve themselves, and there are corrupt actors, but the majority, I believe, want to achieve good. Maybe for the greater good, maybe for personal legacy.

I don't believe in things such as protests. They don't work because the system won't allow it. What might be fair to one group is then unfair to another.

The reason we get here is inherently human. We are bad at understanding the ripples of our decisions. A well-meaning rule designed to fix an issue that is loudly debated today can cause downstream problems three or four orders down the line. So we introduce another rule to fix that consequence, which creates its own downstream impact. And on and on it goes until gridlock is reached.

This is nothing new. Great civilizations in the past have hit this exact wall and slowly crumbled under it. In the Late Roman Republic, so many overlapping vetoes and checks were built to stop any one faction from taking over that the Senate couldn't pass basic reforms, until exhausted citizens welcomed strongmen like Caesar to bypass the rules entirely. 

Later, empires like the Late Ming Dynasty and Late Rome buried themselves under endless layers of protective rules and taxes until the system became too heavy to sustain itself, decaying from within until a minor shock knocked it over. Even in modern history, it took the physical destruction of World War II to clear out entrenched rules in Germany and Japan so they could rebuild from scratch, while un-disrupted winners stalled out in red tape.

Of course, much has changed since then. Humans have evolved, and more than anything, our tools have evolved. Today, and especially in the near future, agentic simulation can help us see the 2nd-, 3rd-, and 4th-order consequences of well-meaning regulations before they are carved into unchangeable law, stripping away the illusion that a policy can ever be fair to all.

This is also the strongest argument for how humans and machines should work together. While a simulation can show the consequences, it can’t (and shouldn’t) make the choice.
Knowing the ripples of a decision forces humans to stop pretending nobody loses, and openly decide who we are choosing to be fair to, for how long, and when it’s someone else’s turn.
