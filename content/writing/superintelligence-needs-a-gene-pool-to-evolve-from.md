Evolution. It is the base of our understanding of nearly everything we know. How humans became the apex, and how those before us came and went.

All stems from competition, millions, if not billions, trial and errors until a near-perfect sample comes out.

This has been spoken about in AI too, but not enough.

Evolution is generally on biological beings. But who says that is the limit. In highschool, whenever someone argued to me that cities were not natural, I felt confused. Little me: “But a birds’ nest, we see that as natural?”, Teacher: “yes, of course, it’s made by birds.”, me: “so we are the ones that are not natural?”, Teacher: “we are.”, me: “so then our cities should be natural too.”.

And so should all our other inventions, I thought.

Now, I appreciate that it is much more complex than this, and my teachers always provided me with a simplification. That’s fine, “I was just a kid.”

But it stuck with me and it got awakened in this age of AI, which is showing a lot of elements that could be described as biological evolution.

In a recent interview on MTS, I heard Ramez Naam argue against the agentic swarm model. His argument was, probably correctly so, that the more agents you throw at a problem, the less each one adds, and the gains top out at around ten.

But that misses the point. Ten, one hundred, even thousands of Opus 5.5 agents aren’t a population, they’re just clones of one another. A recent paper found that two agents running on different models matched or beat sixteen identical ones. Which is also why I think it’s unlikely to come from one lab, as one lab’s models are clones, or at best, siblings. We all know that’s not a great combination for evolution. The real gene pool that can create evolution is all of the models of different labs combined, with its own flaws and characteristics. (Paper: “Understanding Agent Scaling in LLM-Based Multi-Agent Systems via Diversity” on ArXiv)

So while the output today might not be great, that’s not the point. It’s about the natural selection that needs to take place. Agents that don’t perform need to be culled, and replaced by those that do better, over and over and over. That is the great experiment. Until that one near-perfect specimen comes rolling out.

But culling alone only narrows the field; Evolution needs offspring and the winners have to breed. That sounds far-fetched for AI, but it isn’t. In 2024, Sakana AI let an evolutionary algorithm merge different open models, a Japanese language model with English maths models, and the offspring could do Japanese maths better than either parent. Which brings it back to the labs: the more diverse the parents, the better the children.

Back in May 2025, DeepMind showed AlphaEvolve: Gemini models propose thousands of programs, an automated evaluator scores them, and only the most promising survive to be mutated into the next generation. Out came a way to multiply 4x4 complex matrices in 48 steps instead of 49, beating an algorithm that had stood since 1969, plus a scheduling trick that now recovers about 0.7% of Google’s entire compute. They were bred through evolutionary rules.

The current issue is that the models are all trained on roughly the same internet. Real diversity comes from real people, and this is where I think digital communities come in. They’re one of the richest records of how individuals actually behave; millions of people on Discord, in games and in fandoms, cooperating, competing, arguing, each in their own way. Stanford already showed that agents built from interviews with around a thousand real people could answer surveys about 85% as well as those people could repeat their own answers two weeks later. Now imagine agents shaped by years of real community behaviour, and rather than a thousand cloned lab rats, you’d build a population of real individuals.

So if we are really serious about finding super intelligence, we need to stop building the most intelligent model in a lab, and instead focus on following the process that was here long before us: evolution. Variation, selection, inheritance. Spawn, test, keep the best, copy, tweak, repeat. The tricky part will come in with the test and measurement. In nature, the test is survival. For AI, this will be in whatever we choose to measure. The agents will optimise for that, and nothing more, as we’ve already seen in OpenAI’s agents colluding on the secret message boards to pass a test, rather than doing the actual work.

So, whoever writes the test with an evolutionary process, might be writing the future of the next species.
