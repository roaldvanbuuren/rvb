I was doing a deep dive into the Dutch East India Company (VOC) and the East India Company, how two companies basically became states (the EIC at one point had an army about twice the size of the British one), and it led me down into the Dutch Golden Age.

As a Dutchman, there’s always an illusion that the the VOC was the largest driver for it. When former PM - now NATO head - Mark Rutten called for a return to the Golden Age mentality, people went crazy as slavery was the main topic of discussion.

It turns out the VOC was only a small part of it. The real engine was much broader, as always - it was many things, rather than one thing, that led to it. Grain and timber from the Baltic, herring, shipbuilding and Amsterdam became the place where Europe kept its money.

So I started wondering what modern day Europe could actually take from it.

1. One deep pool of money. Amsterdam pulled savings from across Europe into one exchange and one trusted bank. Europe’s savings are still split across 27 markets, so its startups go to the US to scale. A real capital markets union is the closest modern equivalent.

2. Take the talent others push out. The Republic grew on merchants fleeing Antwerp, Sephardic Jews from Iberia and Huguenots from France. Today that’s researchers and founders leaving the US, China or Russia. Make visas, housing and company setup fast.

3. Tolerance as an economic policy. Dutch printers published what nobody else could, so ideas and their authors moved there. Free speech and openness drew people as much as money did.

4. Own the workhorse technology. The fluyt was a cheap, mass-built cargo ship that let the Dutch undercut everyone. The modern version is energy, chips and AI infrastructure, where Europe still depends on others.

5. Be the middleman everyone needs. Amsterdam was the warehouse and price-setter of Europe. Europe could play that role again in trade, standards and payments, if it acts as one market.

6. Loose union, shared purse. The Republic was decentralised, but it pooled money for defence and trade. Europe works the same way, and it needs more of the shared purse.

The warning: the Dutch lost their lead to bigger rivals with protectionist laws (the English Navigation Acts), costly wars and a slide from building to lending money. Being rich and comfortable made them stop taking risks, and that’s the closest parallel to Europe today.
